A spouse who spent years moving from base to base — from Camp Pendleton to Naval Base San Diego to an overseas assignment and back — while a partner served often arrives at divorce with one major question: what happens to the retirement pay that was built during the marriage? The answer involves two separate legal frameworks working at the same time: California community property law and a federal statute called the Uniformed Services Former Spouses' Protection Act. Neither one alone tells the full story.

## The federal framework: USFSPA

Until 1982, federal law actually prevented state courts from dividing military retired pay. The Uniformed Services Former Spouses' Protection Act — 10 U.S.C. § 1408, enacted in 1982 — changed that. USFSPA gives state courts the authority to treat **disposable retired pay** as community property and divide it under whatever state law applies.

In California, that means the time-rule formula. The marital share of the pension is the ratio of years married while the service member was on active duty over total years of service, multiplied by the disposable retired pay. The community share is then divided fifty-fifty between the spouses under Cal. Fam. Code § 2550.

## The 10/10 rule — what it actually does

This is the most misunderstood piece of military divorce law. The 10/10 rule, set out in 10 U.S.C. § 1408(d)(2), does **not** determine whether a spouse is entitled to a share of the military pension. It determines whether DFAS (the Defense Finance and Accounting Service) will pay the former spouse's share **directly**.

The misconception — *"if we weren't married 10 years, I get nothing"* — is false under California law. California community property law applies to any military pension earned during marriage, regardless of length. What changes at the 10-year mark is who writes the check.

When the marriage lasted at least 10 years AND the service member performed at least 10 years of creditable service during the marriage (the "10/10" overlap), DFAS will pay the former spouse directly each month. When the marriage was shorter or the service overlap was less than 10 years, the service member receives the full payment and is responsible for paying the former spouse's share — a less reliable arrangement that can become a collection problem if the service member fails to pay.

## The Mansell limitation

For a spouse who is counting on disability-converted retired pay as part of the divorce settlement, this is the section where the math can change overnight. *Mansell v. Mansell* (1989) held that the portion of military retired pay that a service member waives to receive VA disability benefits is **not** divisible under USFSPA — even if it was originally part of the retirement. The waiver is unilateral on the service member's part.

In practical terms: a service member who, post-divorce, applies for VA disability and elects to convert a portion of their taxable retired pay into non-taxable disability pay can reduce the former spouse's share without the court's involvement. California courts have developed offset mechanisms in some cases, but the underlying federal preemption stands.

## Survivor Benefit Plan (SBP) — the one-year deeming window

A military retiree can elect to provide survivor benefits to a former spouse under the Survivor Benefit Plan. The election must be made within one year of the divorce. If neither party makes the election within that window, the former spouse loses access to SBP coverage permanently — even if the court order said they should have it.

This is one of the most expensive mistakes in military divorce. The cost of SBP is a small reduction in the service member's monthly retired pay; the value to a surviving former spouse can be tens of thousands of dollars per year for life. The one-year window starts running the day the divorce is final.

## CRDP vs CRSC — small acronyms, large consequences

Two relevant federal programs for service members with disability ratings:

**CRDP (Concurrent Retirement and Disability Pay)** — allows certain retirees with VA disability ratings of 50% or higher to receive both retirement pay and disability pay without waiver. Under CRDP, the retirement pay remains divisible.

**CRSC (Combat-Related Special Compensation)** — for service members with combat-related disabilities. Unlike CRDP, CRSC payments are **not** divisible community property. A service member who elects CRSC over CRDP can reduce the former spouse's share.

In some cases, the election between CRDP and CRSC is the most consequential financial decision either party will make post-divorce. It's worth talking through before the judgment is finalized.

## What this means in practice

For a spouse considering divorce from a service member in San Diego, three practical points:

First, the length of marriage doesn't determine entitlement — only the mechanism of payment. The community share exists from day one.

Second, the SBP election must happen within one year of judgment. Don't assume the court order alone is enough; verify the deemed election was filed.

Third, the disability-waiver issue is real and ongoing. A divorce judgment that doesn't address what happens if the service member later applies for VA disability is leaving money on the table.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does the 10/10 rule mean I get nothing if we were married less than 10 years?

No. California community property law applies regardless of marriage length. The 10/10 rule only affects whether DFAS pays you directly each month. For shorter marriages, the service member receives the full payment and is responsible for paying your share — which is enforceable but less reliable than a direct DFAS payment.

### What if the service member is still on active duty?

The pension can still be divided. The community share is calculated based on years married while the service member was on active duty. The actual division typically occurs at retirement; the order goes to DFAS and payments begin when retired pay begins.

### Can the service member reduce my share by going on disability?

Yes, under *Mansell v. Mansell*. The portion of retired pay waived to receive VA disability is not divisible. Some California courts have used offset mechanisms to address this, but federal preemption limits what state courts can do. This is one of the topics worth raising specifically with an attorney.

### What forms do I need?

The most important is the qualifying order — typically called a Military Pension Division Order — which is what DFAS uses to start direct payments. It must include specific language and findings that DFAS requires. Standard QDRO forms don't work.

### Is the Survivor Benefit Plan automatic?

No. The election must be filed within one year of divorce. If the deadline passes without an election, the former spouse loses SBP access permanently. The service member can file a "deemed election" if they were ordered to do so but didn't; the former spouse can also file directly.
