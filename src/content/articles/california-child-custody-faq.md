faq_items:
  - question: "What's the difference between legal custody and physical custody in California?"
    answer: "<p><strong>Legal custody</strong> is the right and responsibility to make significant decisions about a child's health, education, and welfare (Cal. Fam. Code § 3003) — things like school choice, medical treatment, religious upbringing, and major non-emergency decisions. <strong>Physical custody</strong> is where the child actually lives and the day-to-day care arrangement (Cal. Fam. Code § 3004). The two are separate and can be divided differently: joint legal + sole physical is a common arrangement.</p>"
  - question: "What does \"best interest of the child\" actually mean?"
    answer: "<p>The best-interest standard under Cal. Fam. Code § 3011 is the framework California courts use for every custody decision. The statute lists factors: the child's health, safety, and welfare; any history of abuse; the nature and frequency of contact with each parent; and any history of substance abuse. It is not a checklist with point values — judges weigh the factors based on the specific family. In practice, the most weight tends to fall on stability, the existing caregiving pattern, and the absence of safety concerns.</p>"
  - question: "Will the court split custody 50/50 by default?"
    answer: "<p>No — California has no presumption in favor of any particular custody schedule. What courts look for is what serves the child's best interest given the specific facts. In families where both parents have been actively involved and live close enough to make shared parenting practical, 50/50 schedules are common. In families where one parent has been the primary caregiver, equal time is less common.</p>"
  - question: "Do children get to choose which parent they live with?"
    answer: "<p>California law allows the court to consider the preferences of a child who is mature enough to form an intelligent preference (Cal. Fam. Code § 3042). For children 14 or older, the court must allow the child to address the court if the child wishes — though the judge controls how that happens (often through an attorney or a confidential interview). For younger children, the court has discretion to consider their preferences but is not required to.</p>"
  - question: "What is Family Court Services and do I have to go?"
    answer: "<p>Family Court Services (FCS) is the mandatory mediation program for contested custody and visitation cases in California. Before a court will hear a contested custody motion, the parties must attend FCS. In San Diego County, FCS uses a <strong>recommending</strong> model: if the parents don't reach agreement, the mediator submits a written recommendation to the judge. Many California counties use a <strong>non-recommending</strong> model where the mediator only helps the parents negotiate. The recommending model means what you say to FCS carries weight.</p>"
  - question: "Can I modify a custody order later?"
    answer: "<p>Yes. California allows modification of custody orders, but the standard depends on whether you're modifying a final order or a temporary order. Final custody orders require a showing of <strong>significant change in circumstances</strong> since the order was made, plus a showing that modification serves the child's best interest. Temporary orders can usually be modified on a lower standard. The specific facts matter — talk to an attorney about whether your situation meets the standard.</p>"
  - question: "What about the § 3044 domestic violence presumption?"
    answer: "<p>Cal. Fam. Code § 3044 creates a rebuttable presumption that custody to a parent who has committed domestic violence within the preceding five years is detrimental to the best interest of the child. The presumption can be rebutted by a showing that custody to that parent is nonetheless in the child's best interest, based on factors listed in the statute. This presumption is one of the most consequential rules in California custody law — in cases where it applies, it shifts the burden significantly.</p>"
  - question: "What happens if one parent wants to move with the child?"
    answer: "<p>Move-away requests are governed by California case law (notably <em>In re Marriage of Burgess</em> and <em>In re Marriage of LaMusga</em>). The analysis depends on whether the moving parent has sole or joint physical custody, the distance of the move, and how the move affects the child's relationship with the other parent. Move-away cases are complex and fact-specific — they typically involve evaluations and contested hearings.</p>"
  - question: "How long does a contested custody case take in San Diego?"
    answer: "<p>From initial filing to a final custody order, contested cases in San Diego County typically take six to eighteen months. The timeline depends on FCS scheduling, whether a custody evaluation is ordered (which adds three to six months), and whether the case settles or proceeds to trial. Temporary orders are usually in place within sixty to ninety days of filing.</p>"
  - question: "What is a custody evaluation and when does the court order one?"
    answer: "<p>A custody evaluation under California Rule of Court 5.220 is a comprehensive assessment by a qualified mental health professional who interviews both parents, the children, and collateral contacts; observes parent-child interactions; and produces a written report with recommendations. Courts order evaluations in higher-conflict cases or when there are serious concerns (mental health, substance abuse, allegations of abuse). Evaluations cost $5,000 to $15,000 in San Diego County and take three to six months to complete.</p>"
  - question: "Can grandparents get visitation rights?"
    answer: "<p>California allows grandparent visitation under limited circumstances. The most common path is under Cal. Fam. Code § 3104, which permits grandparent visitation when the parents are not married to each other or are separated, and when the court finds a pre-existing bond between the grandparent and child. The bar is higher when both parents are married and oppose visitation.</p>"
  - question: "What's the role of a minor's counsel?"
    answer: "<p>In high-conflict custody cases, the court may appoint a minor's counsel — an attorney who represents the child's interests independently of either parent. Minor's counsel can investigate, interview, attend hearings, and make recommendations to the court. The cost is typically allocated between the parents based on ability to pay. Appointment of minor's counsel signals that the court views the case as complex enough to warrant independent advocacy for the child.</p>"

A parent trying to understand how California courts approach custody is often doing so during one of the most disorienting periods they will face — when the structure of daily family life is uncertain and the stakes feel impossibly high. The questions below are organized around the two frameworks that run through every California custody case: **legal custody**, which is the right to make decisions about a child's health, education, and welfare, and **physical custody**, which is where the child actually lives. All San Diego County custody matters are governed by California law and heard in the Family Law Division of the San Diego Superior Court.

The answers below are general — written by an attorney to help you understand how the law works in California, not to address the specific facts of your case. For those, a consultation is the right next step.
