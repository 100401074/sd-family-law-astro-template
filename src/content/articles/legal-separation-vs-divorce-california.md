Married California residents who are weighing these two paths are often in a specific position: they know they need some kind of legal structure — court-enforceable orders on property, finances, or children — but they are not yet certain whether ending the marriage entirely is the right move, or even whether it is currently possible given residency timing, health insurance, or personal circumstances. Legal separation and divorce are distinct legal proceedings under California law, and the difference between them is more consequential than most people realize before they sit down with an attorney. This article walks through what each path actually does, where they diverge, and the factual scenarios where one tends to fit better than the other.

## The one difference that shapes everything else

The single structural distinction between these two paths is marital status — and it drives every other difference downstream. A judgment of legal separation resolves the practical and financial dimensions of a marriage: it divides property and debts, establishes spousal support, and puts child custody and support orders in place. What it does not do is end the marriage. After a judgment of legal separation, the parties remain legally married.

Divorce — formally called dissolution of marriage in California — does all of the same things, and then terminates the marital relationship entirely. Under Cal. Fam. Code § 2310, dissolution of marriage ends the marriage itself. That one additional step has real consequences: for health insurance eligibility, for the ability to remarry, for certain federal benefits tied to marriage length, and for what happens if one spouse later changes their mind about the proceedings.

Both paths produce court-enforceable orders. Neither is categorically easier or faster in all cases. The right fit depends on the couple's specific circumstances.

## What legal separation actually does

Legal separation is governed by Cal. Fam. Code §§ 2310 and 2345. A judgment of legal separation is a real court judgment — it is not a temporary arrangement or an informal agreement. It divides community property and debts, establishes spousal support if applicable, and puts child custody and support orders in place. Those orders are enforceable in the same way divorce orders are.

What the judgment does not change is the marriage itself. The parties remain legally married after the judgment is entered. That has practical consequences in both directions: a legally separated spouse may, depending on the terms of the employer's health plan, remain eligible for coverage — but neither party can remarry.

One feature of legal separation that is frequently overlooked: California imposes no residency requirement to file a petition for legal separation. The six-month California residency and three-month county residency requirements under Cal. Fam. Code § 2320 apply to divorce, not to legal separation.

## What divorce does, and the six-month rule

California divorce is governed by Cal. Fam. Code §§ 2310 through 2313. California is a no-fault divorce state — irreconcilable differences is the standard ground under Cal. Fam. Code § 2310(a), and neither spouse is required to prove fault or wrongdoing.

The most significant procedural feature of California divorce is the six-month waiting period. Under Cal. Fam. Code § 2339, a divorce judgment cannot become final until six months and one day after the respondent is served with the petition. This waiting period applies whether the case is contested or uncontested.

Residency requirements apply to divorce in a way they do not apply to legal separation. At least one spouse must have lived in California for six months and in the filing county for three months before a divorce judgment can be entered.

## The § 2321 conversion risk

Under Cal. Fam. Code § 2321, if one spouse files for legal separation and the other responds requesting dissolution of marriage instead, the court may grant the divorce. One spouse cannot compel the other to remain in a legal separation proceeding if the other wants a divorce. This is a critical point for anyone considering legal separation as a way to preserve the marriage over a spouse's objection — it does not work that way.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I file for legal separation in California if I just moved here?

Yes. California does not impose a residency requirement to file for legal separation, which distinguishes it from divorce — divorce requires six months of California residency and three months of residency in the filing county under Cal. Fam. Code § 2320. One important caveat: if the other spouse responds to a legal separation petition by requesting divorce instead, the court may grant the divorce under § 2321.

### Does legal separation protect me financially the same way divorce does?

A judgment of legal separation under Cal. Fam. Code § 2345 includes the same types of court-enforceable orders as divorce — property and debt division, spousal support, child custody and support. The key difference is that the marriage itself remains intact. Financial protections are structurally equivalent.

### Can a legal separation be converted to a divorce later?

Yes. Either spouse can request conversion of a legal separation to a divorce by filing the appropriate request with the court. The other spouse cannot block this conversion. Choosing legal separation now does not permanently foreclose divorce later — it preserves optionality in a way that divorce does not.
